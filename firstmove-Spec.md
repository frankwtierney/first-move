# First Move
## Design Spec: Measures, Items, and Results Content

Companion to the LEEP Facilitation & Success Strategies session. This defines what the assessment measures and the full item pool, ready to hand to Claude Code for the build.

---

## 1. What This Measures (and What It Doesn't)

**Construct:** a CA's *default first move* in facilitation moments, under mild time pressure, before deliberate skill kicks in.

**Not measured:** personality, ability, or fit for the role. This is a tendency snapshot, not a type. The results language should say "your default move" and "your instinct," never "you are a ___" as a fixed identity.

**Why situational judgment instead of self-report:** Likert self-report ("I am energized by people: 1-5") is faster to write but measures self-image, and CAs will pattern-match to whatever sounds best for the job. Forced-choice scenarios where all four options are reasonable make the instrument harder to game and keep results tied to behavior in the actual role. It also generates better reflection material: every item is itself a rehearsal of a real CA moment.

**Relationship to DiSC:** the two axes below deliberately parallel the DiSC axes (active/reserved, task/people) so ELP Week 2 can reactivate this experience. Keep DiSC letters out of the CA-facing results. Facilitators can know the mapping; the Week 2 reveal lands better if students discover it there.

---

## 2. The Model

Two axes, crossed:

| | **Moves toward the task** | **Moves toward the person/group** |
|---|---|---|
| **Acts quickly** | **Problem Solver** (fix, decide, advance) | **Group Energizer** (rally, lighten, connect) |
| **Holds back first** | **Analytical Observer** (watch, gather, structure) | **Active Listener** (stay with, validate, steady) |

Each item presents one facilitation moment with four responses, one keyed to each tendency. Score is a simple count across items. Primary tendency = highest count. Report primary and secondary (most CAs are a blend, and the secondary makes the reflection richer).

**Tie handling:** if the top two are tied, report both as a blend ("Solver-Observer") rather than forcing a tiebreaker. Blends are honest and the Menti grid still works: instruct them to submit whichever of the two felt truest reading the results.

---

## 3. Item Design Rules

These matter more than the item count:

1. **Every option must be defensible.** If one answer is obviously "correct CA behavior," the item measures test-savviness. All four should be things a well-meaning person would genuinely do first.
2. **Ask for the FIRST instinct, not the best answer.** Every stem ends with a variant of "what's your first instinct?" and the intro screen says: *there are no right answers here, and answering with what you'd actually do (not what you think you should do) is the only way the result is useful to you.*
3. **Instincts, not outcomes.** Options describe a move ("ask what happened right before"), not a virtue ("show empathy").
4. **Span the role.** Items rotate through CMU group moments, CheckPoint 1-on-1s, roommate dynamics, and informal hallway contact so no tendency gets home-field advantage.
5. **Randomize option order per item** in the build so the same tendency isn't always option A.

**Length:** 12 items, roughly 90 seconds to 2 minutes total. Long enough for a stable-ish signal across contexts, short enough for a live room of 80.

## 4. Item Pool (12 items)

Key: **PS** = Problem Solver, **GE** = Group Energizer, **AL** = Active Listener, **AO** = Analytical Observer. Randomize order in build; keys shown here for scoring only.

**1.** You're in a 1-on-1 CheckPoint. Mid-sentence, the resident trails off and goes quiet. Your first instinct:
- Let the silence sit and stay with them until they pick it back up **(AL)**
- Gently offer a guess at where the sentence was going so they can keep moving **(PS)**
- Warm the moment back up: "hey, no pressure, we've got time" with a smile **(GE)**
- Mentally replay what they said right before, looking for what stopped them **(AO)**

**2.** Your CMU icebreaker just landed flat. The room is silent. Your first instinct:
- Jump in with your own answer, big and a little self-deprecating, to lower the bar **(GE)**
- Rework the prompt on the fly into something easier to answer **(PS)**
- Give the room a beat; someone usually goes first if you can stand the pause **(AL)**
- Scan faces to figure out whether the problem is the prompt, the hour, or the group **(AO)**

**3.** During a Roommate CheckPoint, the two roommates start talking over each other. Your first instinct:
- Stop it and set structure: "one at a time, you first, then you" **(PS)**
- Break the tension with a light comment, then restart the conversation **(GE)**
- Let it run a few more seconds; what they interrupt each other about tells you a lot **(AO)**
- Turn to the one who got cut off first: "finish your thought, I want to hear it" **(AL)**

**4.** A resident says, "My classes are way harder than I thought they'd be." Your first instinct:
- Ask what "harder" means for them: which class, what part **(AO)**
- Tell them about resources: tutoring, the Success Center, office hours **(PS)**
- Reflect it back: "sounds like this semester's hitting different than you expected" **(AL)**
- Normalize it with energy: "honestly, everyone's saying this, you're so not alone" **(GE)**

**5.** Your CMU activity is running long. The group is into it, but you have two steps left. Your first instinct:
- Cut it and move on; the arc matters and the Landing Point can't get squeezed **(PS)**
- Ride the energy a bit longer; engagement like this is the whole point **(GE)**
- Quickly calculate what to trim from the remaining steps to fit both in **(AO)**
- Check the room: ask the group whether they want more time here **(AL)**

**6.** A resident who never talks at CMUs finally speaks, and the thought comes out half-formed and confusing. Your first instinct:
- Nod and give them room to keep going, even if it stays messy **(AL)**
- Enthusiastically grab onto any piece of it: "yes! okay, say more about that" **(GE)**
- Restate it cleanly for the group: "so what you're saying is..." **(PS)**
- Ask one small clarifying question to help them find the thread **(AO)**

**7.** One resident is dominating your CMU discussion. Again. Your first instinct:
- Redirect by name: "let's hear three voices we haven't heard yet" **(PS)**
- Catch the eye of a quieter resident you know has a take, and invite them in warmly **(GE)**
- Wait for the natural pause; interrupting the dominator can chill the whole room **(AL)**
- Track who's checked out and how long this has run before choosing a move **(AO)**

**8.** A resident knocks on your door upset and vents for five straight minutes. When they stop, your first instinct:
- Ask them what they want to happen next **(PS)**
- Say what you heard back to them, feelings included **(AL)**
- Ask a question about the one detail that didn't add up **(AO)**
- Reassure them first: "I'm really glad you came to me with this" **(GE)**

**9.** Your CMU discussion has drifted onto a tangent. It's off-topic, but the group is genuinely connecting. Your first instinct:
- Let it breathe; connection is community, and you can flex the plan **(GE)**
- Bridge it back: find the thread that connects the tangent to the week's focus **(PS)**
- Listen closely to the tangent itself; what a group drifts toward matters **(AL)**
- Weigh time remaining against what's left in the plan before deciding **(AO)**

**10.** You ask a debrief question at your CMU. Ten full seconds of silence. Your first instinct:
- Hold it; ten seconds feels like sixty, and someone is almost always formulating **(AL)**
- Rephrase the question in simpler words **(PS)**
- Answer it partially yourself with some energy to prime the pump **(GE)**
- Consider whether the question came too early in the arc for them to answer yet **(AO)**

**11.** In a Roommate CheckPoint, each roommate gives you a completely different version of the same conflict. Your first instinct:
- Ask each of them questions until the timeline actually lines up **(AO)**
- Name it out loud: "you two are describing different rooms right now," and sit with what that means **(AL)**
- Skip the past: "let's not litigate history, let's agree on what happens from today" **(PS)**
- Find the overlap and build on it: "you both said you want the room to feel less tense" **(GE)**

**12.** A resident looks at you and asks directly: "What should I do?" Your first instinct:
- Tell them what you'd do in their position; they asked, and you have a real answer **(PS)**
- Turn it back: "what are the options as you see them?" **(AO)**
- Slow it down: "before I answer, tell me more about what's pulling at you" **(AL)**
- Boost them first: "honestly, the way you've handled this so far, you're closer than you think" **(GE)**

## 5. Results Screen Content

Show: primary tendency (large), secondary tendency (small), then four blocks. Keep every block tight; this is read live in a room.

### Problem Solver
- **Your default move:** toward the fix. You compress ambiguity into action fast.
- **What it gives you:** momentum. Groups don't stall on your watch, and residents leave conversations with next steps.
- **What it costs you:** ownership. When you fix it, they didn't. Speed can read as not really listening.
- **At a CMU, watch for:** cutting activities short and answering your own debrief questions.
- **At a CheckPoint, watch for:** advice arriving before the resident finishes the story.

### Group Energizer
- **Your default move:** toward the people. You raise the temperature of a room and lower the barrier to joining.
- **What it gives you:** participation. Quiet rooms open up around you, and residents feel liked, fast.
- **What it costs you:** depth. Filling every silence and lightening every heavy moment can keep things at the surface.
- **At a CMU, watch for:** riding fun tangents past the Landing Point.
- **At a CheckPoint, watch for:** reassuring so quickly that the hard thing never fully gets said.

### Active Listener
- **Your default move:** toward staying with it. You hold space, tolerate silence, and let people finish.
- **What it gives you:** trust. Residents tell you things they don't tell anyone else on the floor.
- **What it costs you:** motion. Some moments need an intervention, and holding space can become watching a problem happen.
- **At a CMU, watch for:** letting a dominant voice run because interrupting feels harsh.
- **At a CheckPoint, watch for:** validating in circles when the resident is ready to be asked a harder question.

### Analytical Observer
- **Your default move:** toward understanding first. You gather before you act and see patterns others miss.
- **What it gives you:** accuracy. Your reads on group dynamics and roommate conflicts are usually right.
- **What it costs you:** presence. While you're processing, the room may experience you as absent, and the moment to act can pass.
- **At a CMU, watch for:** managing the plan in your head instead of the people in front of you.
- **At a CheckPoint, watch for:** questions that feel like an investigation instead of a conversation.

**Final screen, after results:** "Now open Mentimeter and place yourself on the grid. Then be ready to answer: at your first CMU and your first CheckPoint, what will you lean on, and where will your instinct need a leash?"

## 6. Build Notes for Claude Code

- Single-page app, no login, no data stored server-side. Nothing leaves the device except what they choose to type into Menti afterward.
- One question per screen, tap to answer, auto-advance, progress bar. Big touch targets; this is 80 people on phones.
- Score client-side. Results render instantly on item 12.
- Randomize option order per item (per session is fine; per user is better).
- Include a restart button and make the URL short/QR-able for the room screen.
- Room-proofing: works on hall wifi or cellular, no external API calls after load.

## 7. Honest Limits (say this much and no more)

This is a locally built reflection tool, not a validated instrument. That's fine for its job, which is generating self-aware application talk, but don't let it drift into anything evaluative: no using results in supervision, placement, or performance conversations, and if a CA pushes back on their result, their pushback is correct by definition. The instrument serves the reflection; the reflection doesn't serve the instrument.

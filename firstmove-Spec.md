# First Move
## Design Spec: Measures, Items, and Results Content

Companion to the LEEP Facilitation & Success Strategies session. This defines what the assessment measures and the full item pool, ready to hand to Claude Code for the build.

---

## 1. What This Measures (and What It Doesn't)

**Construct:** a CA's *default first move* in facilitation moments, under mild time pressure, before deliberate skill kicks in.

**Not measured:** personality, ability, or fit for the role. This is a tendency snapshot, not a type. The results language should say "your primary move" and "your instinct," never "you are a ___" as a fixed identity.

**Why situational judgment instead of self-report:** Likert self-report ("I am energized by people: 1-5") is faster to write but measures self-image, and CAs will pattern-match to whatever sounds best for the job. Forced-choice scenarios where all four options are reasonable make the instrument harder to game and keep results tied to behavior in the actual role. It also generates better reflection material: every item is itself a rehearsal of a real CA moment.

**Relationship to DiSC:** the two axes below deliberately parallel the DiSC axes (active/reserved, task/people) so ELP Week 2 can reactivate this experience. Keep DiSC letters out of the CA-facing results. Facilitators can know the mapping; the Week 2 reveal lands better if students discover it there.

---

## 2. The Model

Two axes, crossed:

| | **Moves toward the task** | **Moves toward the person/group** |
|---|---|---|
| **Acts quickly** | **Problem Solver** (fix, decide, advance) | **Group Energizer** (rally, lighten, connect) |
| **Holds back first** | **Analytical Observer** (watch, gather, structure) | **Active Listener** (stay with, validate, steady) |

On-screen the axes read plainly: *Acts quickly / Holds back first* (vertical) and *The situation / The person* (horizontal).

Each item presents one facilitation moment with four responses, one keyed to each tendency. Score is a simple count across items. Primary tendency = highest count. Report primary and secondary (most CAs are a blend, and the secondary makes the reflection richer).

**Tie handling:** if the top two are tied, report both as a blend ("Solver-Observer") rather than forcing a tiebreaker. Blends are honest and the Menti grid still works: instruct them to submit whichever of the two felt truest reading the results.

---

## 3. Item Design Rules

These matter more than the item count:

1. **Every option must be defensible.** If one answer is obviously "correct CA behavior," the item measures test-savviness. All four should be things a well-meaning person would genuinely do first.
2. **Ask for the FIRST instinct, not the best answer.** Every stem ends with a variant of "what's your first instinct?" and the intro screen says: *there are no right answers here, and answering with what you'd actually do (not what you think you should do) is the only way the result is useful to you.*
3. **Instincts, not outcomes.** Options describe a move ("ask what happened right before"), not a virtue ("show empathy").
4. **Span the role.** Items rotate through CMU group moments, individual CheckPoints (iCPs), Roommate CheckPoints, and informal hallway contact so no tendency gets home-field advantage.
5. **Randomize option order per item** in the build so the same tendency isn't always option A.

**Length:** 13 items, roughly 90 seconds to 2 minutes total. Long enough for a stable-ish signal across contexts, short enough for a live room of 80.

## 4. Item Pool (13 items)

Key: **PS** = Problem Solver, **GE** = Group Energizer, **AL** = Active Listener, **AO** = Analytical Observer. Keys shown here for scoring only; option order is randomized in the build. Item order below is the app's presentation order, deliberately sequenced so similar contexts and tensions (silence/withdrawal, conflict, plan-vs-engagement, distress) never sit adjacent.

**1.** A resident says, "My classes are way harder than I thought they'd be." Your first instinct:
- Ask what "harder" means for them: which class, what part **(AO)**
- Tell them about resources: tutoring, the Success Center, office hours **(PS)**
- Reflect it back: "sounds like this semester's hitting different than you expected" **(AL)**
- Normalize it with energy: "honestly, everyone's saying this, you're so not alone" **(GE)**

**2.** One resident is dominating your CMU discussion. ...Again. Your first instinct:
- Redirect by name: "let's hear three voices we haven't heard yet" **(PS)**
- Catch the eye of a quieter resident you know has a take, and invite them in warmly **(GE)**
- Wait for the natural pause; interrupting the dominator can chill the whole room **(AL)**
- Track who's checked out and how long this has run before choosing a move **(AO)**

*(App displays "...Again." on its own line.)*

**3.** You're in an individual CheckPoint (iCP). The resident was opening up, but their answers are getting shorter and more closed off. Your first instinct:
- Let it get quiet and stay with them — don't rush to fill the space **(AL)**
- Try a different, easier question — something concrete to get them talking again **(PS)**
- Warm it back up with some lightness: "hey, no pressure, we've got time" **(GE)**
- Replay what shifted — what you'd just asked when they started pulling back **(AO)**

**4.** Your CMU discussion has drifted onto a tangent. It's off-topic, but the group is genuinely connecting. Your first instinct:
- Let it breathe; connection is community, and you can flex the plan **(GE)**
- Bridge it back: find the thread that connects the tangent to the week's focus **(PS)**
- Listen closely to the tangent itself; what a group drifts toward matters **(AL)**
- Weigh time remaining against what's left in the plan before deciding **(AO)**

**5.** You're in a Roommate CheckPoint. One roommate is fine with frequent guests — even overnight, no heads-up needed — and figures the other feels the same. The other isn't comfortable with it. Both are polite, but stuck. Your first instinct:
- Get specific with both: how often, how much notice, which nights — so the real gap is clear **(AO)**
- Name what's underneath: one wants an open door, the other wants a heads-up — both are fair **(AL)**
- Steer them toward one ground rule they can both live with, like a quick text before guests **(PS)**
- Anchor on what they share: you both want the room to feel comfortable to come back to **(GE)**

> *Item-5 rationale:* reframed away from "different versions of the same conflict." Roommate CheckPoints happen early, before conflicts harden; the truer RCP moment is two residents discovering their habits/values don't line up (here, guests) and working, politely, toward a shared agreement.

**6.** You're partway through an iCP when the resident starts venting — five straight minutes pour out. Then they stop. Your first instinct:
- Ask them what they want to happen next **(PS)**
- Say what you heard back to them, feelings included **(AL)**
- Gently ask about the one part that didn't quite fit **(AO)**
- Reassure them first: "I'm really glad you came to me with this" **(GE)**

**7.** You ask a debrief question at your CMU. Ten full seconds of silence. Your first instinct:
- Hold it; ten seconds feels like sixty, and someone is almost always formulating **(AL)**
- Rephrase the question in simpler words **(PS)**
- Answer it partially yourself with some energy to prime the pump **(GE)**
- Consider whether the question came too early in the arc for them to answer yet **(AO)**

**8.** You're in an iCP and the resident keeps insisting everything's "fine, good, no complaints" — but nothing about them reads fine. Your first instinct:
- Gently name the gap: "you're saying fine, but something feels off — tell me if I'm wrong" **(AL)**
- Give them an easy on-ramp: "one thing, big or small, that's been rattling around lately?" **(PS)**
- Warm it up so the wall comes down: "no agenda here, I actually just want to know how you are" **(GE)**
- Read the tells — tone, eye contact, what they skip past — before you decide your move **(AO)**

> *Item-8 rationale:* replaced an earlier flat-icebreaker → silence prompt that was a near-duplicate of the debrief-silence item (item 7) — same four moves (wait / simplify / self-answer / diagnose) — and over-weighted CMU contexts. This reading-past-a-deflection moment is a distinct iCP skill and rebalances the item pool.

**9.** Your CMU activity is running long. The group is into it, but you have two steps left. Your first instinct:
- Cut it and move on; the arc matters and the Landing Point can't get squeezed **(PS)**
- Ride the energy a bit longer; engagement like this is the whole point **(GE)**
- Quickly calculate what to trim from the remaining steps to fit both in **(AO)**
- Check the room: ask the group whether they want more time here **(AL)**

**10.** You're in a Roommate CheckPoint and the two roommates start talking over each other. Your first instinct:
- Stop it and set structure: "one at a time, you first, then you" **(PS)**
- Break the tension with a light comment, then restart the conversation **(GE)**
- Let it run a few more seconds; what they interrupt each other about tells you a lot **(AO)**
- Turn to the one who got cut off first: "finish your thought, I want to hear it" **(AL)**

**11.** You're in an iCP and the resident is talking plenty — but none of it maps to the areas you're there to cover on your tracker. Your first instinct:
- Steer them back toward the areas you're there to check in on **(PS)**
- Let them keep going; they're talking and connecting, and that's the real win **(GE)**
- Follow what they actually want to talk about — that IS the check-in **(AL)**
- Clock the mismatch first: are they steering around your areas, or do the areas just not fit them? **(AO)**

> *Item-11 rationale:* over-sharing that misses the tracker areas is a common iCP moment and probes a distinct tension — does the conversation serve the form, or the form serve the conversation. It rhymes with the plan-vs-connection pull in the CMU pacing and tangent items (items 9 and 4) but lands it in a 1-on-1, learner-centered frame rather than a group-pacing one.

**12.** A resident who never talks at CMUs finally speaks, and the thought comes out half-formed and confusing. Your first instinct:
- Nod and give them room to keep going, even if it stays messy **(AL)**
- Enthusiastically grab onto any piece of it: "yes! okay, say more about that" **(GE)**
- Restate it cleanly for the group: "so what you're saying is..." **(PS)**
- Ask one small clarifying question to help them find the thread **(AO)**

**13.** You're deep in an iCP. The resident has been thinking out loud for a while, then looks right at you and asks: "What should I do?" Your first instinct:
- Tell them what you'd do in their position; they asked, and you have a real answer **(PS)**
- Turn it back: "what are the options as you see them?" **(AO)**
- Slow it down: "before I answer, tell me more about what's pulling at you" **(AL)**
- Boost them first: "honestly, the way you've handled this so far, you're closer than you think" **(GE)**

## 5. Results Screen Content

**Grid-explainer screen (shown after the last item, before the result):** the same empty 2×2 the CA will stand on in Mentimeter, with the two axes in plain language so the reveal lands on a grid they already understand. Language stays dead simple — anyone should read it once and get it.
- Eyebrow: *Before your result* · Title: **How to read your grid**
- *Top to bottom — how fast you act:* up top, people do something right away; lower down, people hang back and take the situation in first.
- *Left to right — what you go to first:* on the left, people go straight to fixing the problem; on the right, people go straight to the person in front of them.
- Note: "Your answers place you in one of these four squares. The next screen shows which one — what that instinct does well, and where it can trip you up." → **Show me**

**Results screen:** the primary move (large tendency name), the next move (small), then five blocks. Blocks use light inline emphasis (*italic*); the one-word answer after *What it gives you* / *What it costs* renders as an italic lead. Language stays plain and descriptive — no jargon like "hold space." Keep every block tight; this is read live in a room.

### Problem Solver
- **In the moment:** You go straight for the solution. When something's unresolved, you turn it into a clear next step, fast.
- **What it gives you:** *Momentum.* Groups don't stall on your watch, and residents leave a conversation knowing what to do next.
- **What it costs:** *Ownership.* When *you* solve it, *they* didn't — and the lesson that sticks is the one a resident works out on their own, not the one you hand them. Moving fast can also come across as not really listening.
- **At a CMU, watch for:** cutting activities short and answering your own debrief questions. A debrief works when the *residents* reach the point themselves — that's where the learning happens. Answer it for them and you've taken away the thinking they came to do. Ask, then wait.
- **At a CheckPoint, watch for:** offering advice, referrals, or resources before the resident has finished sharing. Knowing the right service or person to point them to is essential — but *when* and *how* you offer it matters just as much. A referral isn't a box to check; it's a form of care that shows you understood their situation first.

### Group Energizer
- **In the moment:** You go straight to the people. You lift the energy in a room and make it easy for residents to jump in.
- **What it gives you:** *Participation.* Quiet rooms open up around you, and residents warm to you quickly.
- **What it costs:** *Depth.* Filling every silence and lightening every heavy moment keeps things on the surface — and the hard thing a resident *almost* said usually needed the room to stay quiet long enough for them to say it.
- **At a CMU, watch for:** riding a fun tangent past the Landing Point. The energy is the *warm-up*, not the goal — if the group never reaches the takeaway, they had a good time and can't name what they learned.
- **At a CheckPoint, watch for:** reassuring so fast that the hard thing never actually gets said. Sometimes the most helpful move is to let a silence sit instead of filling it.

### Active Listener
- **In the moment:** You slow down and give the resident room. You let silences sit, let people finish, and make it safe for them to keep talking.
- **What it gives you:** *Trust.* Residents tell you things they don't tell anyone else on the floor.
- **What it costs:** *Movement.* Some moments need you to step in, and staying quiet and patient can slip into just watching a problem unfold. Understanding a situation isn't the same as helping it change.
- **At a CMU, watch for:** letting one loud voice run because cutting in feels rude. Protecting one person's comfort can cost the other ten their turn — guiding the room is part of caring for it.
- **At a CheckPoint, watch for:** staying in reassurance when the resident is ready to go a little deeper. Gently naming what you're noticing can open the conversation up, not shut it down.

### Analytical Observer
- **In the moment:** You take it in before you act. You watch, gather the details, and notice patterns other people miss.
- **What it gives you:** *Accuracy.* Your read on a group's mood or a roommate conflict is usually right.
- **What it costs:** *Presence.* While you're still thinking, the room can feel like you've checked out — and a resident won't open up to someone who's gone quiet inside their own head. The moment to act can pass while you're still sizing it up.
- **At a CMU, watch for:** running the plan in your head instead of reading the people in front of you. The plan is a guide, not the goal — facilitate the room, not the outline.
- **At a CheckPoint, watch for:** asking so many questions it starts to feel like an interview instead of a conversation. A resident can tell the difference between being *understood* and being *studied*.

**Final screen, after results:** "Now open Mentimeter and place yourself on the grid. Then be ready to answer: at your first CMU and your first CheckPoint, what will you lean on, and where will your instinct need a leash?"

## 6. Build Notes for Claude Code

- Single-page app, no login, no data stored server-side. Nothing leaves the device except what they choose to type into Menti afterward.
- One question per screen, tap to answer, auto-advance, progress bar. Big touch targets; this is 80 people on phones.
- Score client-side. The final item goes to the grid-explainer screen (§5); the result renders on the tap after it.
- Randomize option order per item (per session is fine; per user is better).
- Include a restart button and make the URL short/QR-able for the room screen.
- Room-proofing: works on hall wifi or cellular, no external API calls after load.

## 7. Honest Limits (say this much and no more)

This is a locally built reflection tool, not a validated instrument. That's fine for its job, which is generating self-aware application talk, but don't let it drift into anything evaluative: no using results in supervision, placement, or performance conversations, and if a CA pushes back on their result, their pushback is correct by definition. The instrument serves the reflection; the reflection doesn't serve the instrument.
